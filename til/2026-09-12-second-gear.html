Critical / Autonomy The State of Autonomous Trucks in 2026

Pre-read: Autonomous trucking is starting to look less like a pure driving-stack race and more like an operations-system race. Aurora's Hirschbach plan is still non-binding, but the official release says the carrier intends to own 500 Aurora Driver-powered trucks with deliveries beginning in 2027.

Summary: Chris Paxton's essay argues that highway trucking looked deceptively simple because the road is structured, but the failure modes are harsher than city robotaxis: high speeds, long stopping distances, blocked-lane emergencies, roadside-warning rules, and fleet operations without a human in the cab. The useful frame is the split between three routes to commercialization: Aurora's redundancy-heavy Waymo-style stack, constrained environments such as ports and industrial hauling, and Waabi's simulation-first Physical AI platform. Waabi's own January announcement said its $750M Series C plus Uber milestone investment would fund autonomous trucking and a robotaxi expansion, which makes the sector's strategy question sharper: scale may come from shared autonomy primitives, but deployment still depends on boring compliance, customer operations, and recovery workflows.

Happenings / Robotics Robot pets still need a buyer

Pre-read: Social robots have an unusually visible value problem: the emotional experience can be real while the hardware business remains fragile. New York's Office for the Aging says its ElliQ initiative has about 900 units available and reported a 95% loneliness reduction in a 2023 study.

Summary: Andra Keay's piece argues that companion robots keep falling between toy economics, therapeutic procurement, platform strategy, and entertainment. Cheap animatronic pets can scale when the job is narrow and subsidized; expensive interactive robots need subscriptions, support, and measurable outcomes. The most founder-useful point is that small robots may work as developer platforms, data interfaces, or curated experiences before they work as mass-market emotional hardware. For embodied AI, charm is the demo; maintenance, distribution, and payer logic are the company.

Happenings / Hardware Simulation still needs physical truth

Pre-read: Engineering simulation earns trust through bounded assumptions, measured validation, and repeated use inside design loops. That remains true even when AI or math breakthroughs make the underlying equations feel newly unsettled.

Summary: Hardware FYI uses the Navier-Stokes debate to separate pure-math existence questions from day-to-day CFD practice. Engineers solve finite-grid, finite-time approximations, then compare them against wind tunnels, test rigs, and field data. The issue's best signal is broader than fluids: physical AI, manufacturing models, and robotics simulation all face the same epistemic constraint. A model is useful when it narrows the experiment space, not when it replaces reality.
