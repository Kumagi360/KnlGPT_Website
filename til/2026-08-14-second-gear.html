Trafficking Crackdown Needs Operational Capacity

Aggregation

World

Critical / Reuters Trump's promised sex-trafficking crackdown fails to materialize

Pre-read: Federal trafficking cases depend on victims trusting investigators long enough to sustain difficult prosecutions; DOJ itself describes anti-trafficking work as a multi-component system involving prosecutors, the FBI, victim services, grant funding, training, and local partnerships. DOJ's public framework puts victim services and multidisciplinary coordination beside prosecution, while a NIJ practitioner note stresses that cases should be victim-centered without making survivors carry the entire courtroom burden. That makes staffing, grants, and immigration enforcement priorities central to whether cases can move.

Summary: Reuters interviewed 25 former prosecutors, current agents, and victim advocates, then checked federal court records. It found federal sex-trafficking charges through June fell to 73 people, the slowest first-half pace since 2010 and about 22% below the average for the same period over the prior three years. Related charges also weakened: child-pornography cases were down 3%, and Mann Act prosecutions were down 18%.

The reporting ties the decline to agent reassignments, departed prosecutors, funding cuts, and victims' growing fear of the federal government. Former officials describe investigations freezing when Homeland Security Investigations agents are pulled toward immigration enforcement. DOJ disputes the scale of the decline but did not give Reuters numbers that reproduced its claim. The piece matters because it turns a moral priority claim into an operational audit: budgets, personnel, and survivor support appear to be deciding enforcement capacity more than rhetoric.

Happenings ETF giants lose flow dominance

Pre-read: ETF inflows are still running at record scale, so share loss at the top is a competition story rather than a demand slump. State Street's midyear flow review said first-half 2026 inflows crossed $1 trillion for the first time, with active ETFs taking 39% of year-to-date flows and low-cost ETFs taking 49%. That backdrop makes issuer fragmentation more important for asset managers than for ETF adoption itself.

Summary: Seeking Alpha reports that BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street once captured roughly 80% of ETF industry inflows at their peak six years ago. Their combined share has fallen to about 55%, even as the pool of annual inflows is around $2 trillion. The Big Three still capture enormous absolute dollars, but the marginal ETF buyer is spreading assets across more issuers.

The pressure comes from mutual-fund companies converting strategies into ETFs, new active managers entering the wrapper, and investors looking beyond broad beta products. The article's useful point is structural: the ETF wrapper has become a distribution layer for many strategies, so scale alone is less decisive than it was when passive core funds defined the category. For BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street, the risk is fee pressure plus slower control of the next product cycle.
