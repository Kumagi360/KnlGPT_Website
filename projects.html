Edge-Corrected Teleoperation

A way to make remote robot control less brittle when the connection is slow or a handoff is slightly off.

Project premise

The person keeps the call. The robot handles small corrections close to the work.

  • ProblemLag and messy demonstrations
  • Where it runsOn the robot
  • JobSmall, safe corrections

Remote teleoperation is never as clean as a replay. There is delay, a camera view that arrives late, and plenty of little inputs that only make sense in context. I am working on a local layer that can spot the intended motion and make a small adjustment before the robot acts on it.

How it works

The operator still sends the same pose or velocity commands. A small model on the robot reads recent commands alongside fresh joint, force, and camera data. If it is confident, it adds a limited correction. If it is not, it leaves the operator’s command alone.

The useful part is the logging. Raw commands, the local correction, and what happened next are saved together. That makes it possible to train on the moments where a person was compensating for bad latency, rather than treating every recorded motion as perfect.

operator command
  → local robot state
  → small correction, if useful
  → move and log the result
Remote input→Local check→Robot motion

Swarming Formations

Small mobile parts that could become an end effector one day and a temporary structure the next.

Project premise

Build the shape you need, then pull it apart when you are done.

  • ToolsGripper, scoop, and more
  • StructuresBraces, supports, simple frames
  • Current ideaSoft cubes, flywheels, electromagnets

The interesting bit is not just a gripper that changes shape. The same set of parts might make a scoop, a support under a load, or a rough frame around something that needs holding in place. A single robot body is good at one thing. A pile of parts has a chance to be useful in more situations.

Current direction

I am playing with small cubes that have soft outer surfaces, flywheels inside, and electromagnets on their faces. In theory, each one moves itself and snaps into place with the others. The right shape and way of moving are still open questions. Cubes are just the first thing I am trying.

The first tests are simple: can a few parts form a two-finger gripper, then rearrange into a scoop? Can they also make a stiff enough brace or small support? That means getting the joins right, checking how much load they take, and seeing what happens when one part does not quite land where it should.

move a few parts
  → bring them together
  → test a tool or support
  → take it apart and try again
Move→Connect→Make a tool or structure

Stigmergic Swarms

A swarm that communicated through its environment, using bubbles as an underwater message bus.

Project premise

If robots cannot talk to each other, can they still coordinate by changing the world around them?

  • AgentsMobile robots with no direct link
  • MediumBubbles and dyed fluid in water
  • Vision runtime1,000 ms → 150 ms

At Cornell’s Collective Embodied Intelligence Lab, under Kirstin Petersen, I explored stigmergy: coordination through persistent changes to a shared environment. Bees leave pheromone trails in air. I wanted to know what that idea could look like in water, where connectivity is unreliable and the environment can carry a signal.

The system

I built the full stack around small wheeled agents: a 3D-printed body, two drive wheels and a caster, a fiducial marker, motors, and a snorkel for releasing bubbles or dyed fluid. The agents had no radio link to one another or to the computer. An overhead vision system read a snapshot of the pool, tracked every marker, interpreted the traces in the water, and relayed the resulting state through a multithreaded TCP/IP command-and-data layer with a live query and visualization interface.

The bubble pattern was the message. Its timing, speed, and formation could represent a bit, a location, a direction of travel, or another shared state. I wrote the distributed processing and vision algorithms that made that loop usable, including image segmentation and tracking work that reduced processing time from roughly one second to 150 milliseconds. With that feedback loop in place, the agents showed meaningful multi-agent behavior without ever receiving a direct message from each other.

camera reads the water + agent markers
  → vision stack infers shared state
  → bubble pattern carries a message
  → swarm responds through the environment
Agent makes a trace→Vision reads the pool→Swarm updates behavior

Subsea Sim

A photorealistic training ground for underwater vehicles working around an offshore rig before vessel time got expensive.

Project premise

The simulator made underwater autonomy answer to both what a camera saw and what the water did to a vehicle.

  • OriginEntrepreneur First
  • PrototypeOffshore-rig construction cell
  • StatusShared with offshore engineering teams

The idea started at Entrepreneur First, then I kept working on it after leaving. I built a small digital twin of an offshore rig where underwater vehicles could inspect, line up, and place construction components. A few leading offshore engineering firms saw it and got excited. It is not in my day-to-day now, but I would happily pick it back up.

What I built

The sim used rig and survey geometry, ray-traced lighting, and underwater camera, sonar, and DVL models. I used controllable synthetic data to vary water clarity, lighting, sensor placement, and the state of the components while keeping the labels needed to train perception.

The point was not to make an ocean scene look nice. I wanted a perception stack to deal with murky water, backscatter, changing light, currents, and a vehicle that did not stay perfectly still. The simulator produced RGB, depth, sonar, DVL, and pose data together, then ran the perception work in the loop with the vehicle controls.

  → calibrated RGB / sonar / DVL data
  → perception + state estimation stack
  → current-aware vehicle control
  → inspect, align, place
Rig digital twin→Multimodal perception→Construction task

Visions

Two location-based AR and VR experiences that made Montenegro's history feel less like a plaque and more like a place you could step into.

Project premise

VR can take you anywhere. I wanted it to take you 500 years back from exactly where you were standing.

  • ExperiencesKotorAR + VantageVR
  • LaunchOculus Quest APK for paying visitors
  • PartnersLocal operators + Montenegro NTO

I built Visions while living in Montenegro between high school and my second year of college. COVID had emptied the usual ways of encountering a place, while tourism, more than 30% of Montenegro's GDP, needed better digital infrastructure. I was already deep into AR and VR, and history had always been the part of travel I cared about most. This was the bridge between them.

What made it work

VantageVR did not drop visitors into a generic historical scene. In Perast, visitors put on the headset facing the same waterfront they had just been looking at, then saw the Battle of Perast and the town around them as it could have appeared more than five centuries earlier.

For VantageVR, I worked with an asset developer to turn the researched story and scene design into a Unity experience packaged as an APK for Oculus Quest. The experience was launched for paying customers in Perast and aboard boats travelling to Our Lady of the Rocks. I validated the reconstruction through sustained work with local officials and museum curators, plus a lot of primary and secondary research on Perast.

KotorAR extended the idea to a phone. Visitors could hold up a phone at an attraction, see an AR reconstruction of its earlier form, then follow guides and context into the story around it. I owned the product and technology architecture around the app, including local-business and mass-transit integrations. The asset developer built the AR scenes; I made sure the experience connected those scenes to the city around them.

The project was backed by Montenegro's national tourism organization and was discussed in the country's Senate during the COVID era as part of the conversation around tourism technology infrastructure. I also ran feature iteration, customer and partner feedback, and the material needed to explain the product to funders and operators.

  → Unity scene + Oculus Quest APK
  → on-site VR recreation for visitors

attraction + city data
  → AR scene + guide context
  → business and transit connections
Place + history→AR / VR reconstruction→Visitor context

Humanoid Joint Control

The electronics and firmware that helped Tik Tok keep its joints in step.

Project premise

If the joints are not on the same page, the rest of the robot has no chance.

  • ScopeJoint-control subsystem
  • System10+ coordinated actuation boards
  • FocusEncoder accuracy + real-time timing

Tik Tok is an active biohybrid humanoid project at Dynamic Locomotion, so I can only share the slice I worked on: the joint boards. I designed and fabricated PCBs that read rotary encoders at the knees, hips, and ankles, with elbows planned for the same system.

What the boards did

They turned joint position into low-latency feedback and shared it over CANOpen with the Nvidia Jetson handling onboard compute.

On the firmware side, I worked on real-time control and a distributed scheduler for coordinated rollouts across more than ten actuation boards. The boards used fault-tolerant CANOpen and EtherCAT firmware on an ARM Cortex-M7 SAM microcontroller, with the practical aim of keeping a whole body in sync when one joint was not the only thing moving.

The payoff was seeing a multi-joint rollout start to resemble a gait. Nothing glamorous about a clean encoder trace, but it is a good day when the whole stack finally agrees on where the body is.

rotary encoder
  → Cortex-M7 embedded control
  → CANOpen / EtherCAT network
  → Nvidia Jetson coordination
  → multi-joint gait rollout
Encoder feedback→Distributed joint control→Gait rollout

Cornell Electric Vehicles

A student racecar team where I spent four years building, breaking, and eventually leading the technical side.

Project premise

Make the car smarter, but never make it harder to trust.

  • RoleTechnical Full-Team Lead
  • TeamCornell Electric Vehicles
  • Tenure2020–2024

I started as the Electrical Automation project lead, became Electrical Lead, then Technical Full-Team Lead. While my co-lead handled recruiting and business, I kept the technical build moving as the team grew from 20 people to 60 and took on autonomy alongside the EV program.

On the car

We got Level 1 autonomous emergency braking working and validated it with the actual vehicle.

Most of my time went into two boards. One sat between an onboard Jetson and the motor controller, so autonomy could ask for control without blocking the driver from taking it back. I designed the PCB and the safety handoff around it. I also built several versions of a 2 kW BLDC controller. One of them took a roughly 150 kg car and driver around the Indy circuit, which was a pretty decent test plan.

We also got the early autonomy stack and data collection off the ground: RTK GPS, LiDAR, stereo cameras, SLAM, and object detection on a Jetson. Later teams took that further into Level 2 work. I am very happy I got to build some of the scaffolding.

Jetson autonomy commands
  → CRC + safety checks
  → actuation handoff board
  → motor controller / driver fallback
Jetson commands→Safety handoff→Vehicle actuation

A 12-person high-school team that took a tiny combustion car to Shell Eco-marathon Europe, then spent every lap looking for another drop of efficiency.

Project premise

Efficiency is a whole-car problem. The chassis, engine map, driver, and racing line all get a vote.

  • RoleTeam Lead
  • Team12 students
  • ResultTop high-school efficiency result

I led a 12-person team through Shell Eco-marathon Europe, building a high-efficiency internal-combustion vehicle. We still hold one of the strongest efficiency results recorded by a high-school team in the competition.

On the car

I worked on the carbon-fiber layup for the chassis, then moved between the GX35 Honda engine and the track. That meant mapping and tuning the engine, then writing software to compare driving lines and find the route around the circuit that cost the least energy.

SAFE

A sensor pod and app built to catch heat stress early, before it became a bigger problem.

Project premise

A health product has to survive real homes, not just a clean demo.

  • BuiltSensor pod + AI model
  • PilotIndia field test
  • ResearchANN papers

SAFE came from a simple problem: heat stress does not wait for a controlled experiment. We paired an environmental sensor pod with an app and a model that could flag when conditions were heading somewhere bad.

The hard part

Homes are messy. The system needed to admit when it was unsure instead of pretending every reading added up to one neat score.

The goal was a useful heads-up, not a fancy classifier. Pick up the context, make a call, and show someone what to do next.

signal + context
  → estimate risk
  → surface confidence
  → prompt next action

The India pilot and related ANN research were a reality check: would it still help when data quality, homes, and daily routines looked nothing like a tidy dataset?

Sensor pod→AI model→Care signal
