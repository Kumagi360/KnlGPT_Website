Edge-Corrected Teleoperation

Adjusting joint rollouts on-system to align with intent, rather than relying on tracking accuracy

Project premise

Teleoperation is brittle with feedback latency and sensitive to control resolution

  • ProblemLag and imperfect instructions
  • HardwareJetson Nano Super
  • PurposeCorrections and safety adherence

Remote teleoperation is never as clean as a replay. There is delay, a camera view that arrives late, and plenty of little inputs that only make sense in context. I am working on a local layer that can spot the intended motion and make a small adjustment before the robot acts on it.

The concept

The teleoperator can continue to use inexpensive operating surfaces, relaying pose and joint velocity commands. However, a small model local to the robot processes these commands against fresh joint, force, and camera data from the robot. For repeatable actions, the intent is likely sufficiently captured to produce a local high confidence rollout. If this rollout disagrees with the teleoperated commands, or breaks a safety policy, the model can augment actuiation commands with a limited correction.

In addition to potentially improving performance, the raw commands, local correction, and final rollout are logged together. This makes it possible to train on moments where a person was compensating for bad latency, rather than treating every recorded motion as perfect. As an 'inverse corrective action', a future full-featured supervisor/control model can be trained off a corpus of such corrections.

operator command   → local state check  → intention or safety alignment comparison   → augmented operator command -> local logged result

Swarming Formations

Modular swarm networked units with orientation-control and deformable sensing skin

Project premise

End effectors and structures have narrowly defined utility at design-time. If intelligence is generalizing, why shouldn't the morphology?

  • ConfigurationsGripper, scoop, platform, etc
  • StructuresBraces, supports, simple frames
  • compsitionSoft cubes, flywheels, electromagnets

The same set of subcomponents could make a scoop, a support under a load, or a scaffolding. A single robot body is good at one thing, but having a cluster of components with ability to rearrange with orientation adjustments could generalize aspects of otherwise fixed robot layouts.

Current direction

I've prototyped 1cm^3 small cubes with soft outer surfaces, micro-flywheels, and electromagnets on faces. In theory, each can reorient itself and selectively adhere/repel into place among others others. The right packaging and method of central coordination are still open questions. A little far fetched, but exciting as a hardware experiment.

Shrinking a meaningful inductive charging solution is proving tricky. Getting the joins right, characterising how much load each entity can take, and seeing what happens when parts don't interact correctly will determine if this project has a future.

Stigmergic Swarms

Mobile robot swarms using a fluid surface as a messaging board

Project premise

If robots can't directly communicate, can they coordinate by marking their shared environment?

  • AgentsPartially submerged, inverted, wheeled rovers
  • MediumBubbles / dyes on water
  • vocabulary∞

At Cornell’s Collective Embodied Intelligence Lab, under Kirstin Petersen, I explored stigmergy: coordination through persistent changes to a shared environment. Bees leave pheromone trails in air. I wanted to know what that idea could look like in water, where connectivity is unreliable and the environment can carry a signal.

The system

I helped build the full stack around inexpensive wheeled agents: a 3D-printed body, two driven wheels, identity marker, snorkel for releasing bubbles / dyed fluid, and a fan for directing the emission. The agents had no method of communication with each other, only sandboxed link to a walled controller for individual agent maneuvers. Mounted under the water tank was a camera and traditional vision stack: reading a snapshot of the pool, tracking every marker, interpreting the traces in the water, and relaying the resulting state through a live query and visualization interface.

The bubble pattern was the message. Its timing, speed, and formation could represent a bit, a location, a direction of travel, or another shared state. I wrote the distributed processing and vision algorithms that made that loop usable, including image segmentation and tracking work that reduced processing time from roughly one second to 150 milliseconds. With that feedback loop in place, the agents showed meaningful multi-agent behavior without ever receiving a direct message from each other.

While the outcome of this particular investigation was incorporated into a larger stream of research yet to be published, the project was a first exposure to swarm robotics and the potential for low-cost high-volume robots to be placed in harsh or restrictive conditions.

Subsea Sim

Photoreal closed-loop for underwater robots and complex underwater scenes

Project premise

ROV to UAV transition lags in part due to inadequate sim tooling

  • OriginEntrepreneur First
  • PrototypeOffshore-rig construction cell
  • StatusDemo'ed to offshore engineering teams

The idea started at Entrepreneur First, then I kept working on it after leaving. I built a small digital twin of an offshore rig where underwater vehicles could inspect, lay, and operate on the seabed.

Why now

Subsea robotics applications have seen massive growth in recent years due to an explosion in offshore construction, surveillance, and inspection projects, particular in Asia-Pacific. Yet, these projects remain tremendously expensive due to the need for a surface 'spread' of vessels, hosting remotely operated vehicles (ROV) and their scarce highly trained pilots. These spreads lack operational flexibility, and are bottlenecked by umbilical-based teleoperation methods in water. Unmanned vehicles may be the answer, but require a sim as capable as those available for terrestrial or aerial vehicles in order to be developed and deployed. I've since shown a few leading offshore engineering firms this project, to much excitement about potential productionization.

The point was not to make an ocean scene look nice. I wanted a perception stack to deal with murky water, backscatter, changing light, currents, and a vehicle that did not stay perfectly still. The simulator produced RGB, depth, sonar, DVL, and pose data together, then ran the perception work in the loop with the vehicle controls.

rig CAD + survey geometry + water conditions
  → calibrated RGB / sonar / DVL data
  → perception + state estimation stack
  → current-aware vehicle control
  → ROS integration for engineering assets to be loaded and actutated

Visions

Generalizable mixed reality offerings to make historical and cultural tourism more immersive

Project premise

Why rely on imagination, when we can render historical scenes onto the real world, to be experienced right where they happened?

  • ExperiencesCityAR + VantageVR
  • LaunchedMontenegro, Summer 2021
  • PartnersLocal operators + Montenegro NTO

I built Visions while returning to Kotor in Montenegro between the end of high school and my second year of college. COVID had emptied the historic old-town, with cruise ships and buses from up and down the Balkans missing. Tourism, accounting for 30%+ of Montenegro's GDP, needed its attractions to stand out more in order to attract a limited crowd. I was already deep into AR/VR at the time, and experiencing history had always been the part of travel I cared about most.

What made it work

VantageVR was not designed as an in-home experience. In the Naval Museum at Perast visitors put on the headset facing the waterfront stretched out in front of them. They experienced the 1654 Battle of Perast, with Ottoman sea forces attacking the Venetian town around them, with entities and structures recreated and overlaid as they would have been centuries ago.

For VantageVR, I worked with an asset developer to turn the researched story and scene design into a Unity experience packaged as an APK for Oculus Quest. The experience was launched for paying customers in Perast and aboard boats travelling to Our Lady of the Rocks. I validated the reconstruction through sustained work with local officials and museum curators, plus a lot of primary and secondary research on Perast.

KotorAR extended the idea to a phone. Visitors could hold up a phone at an attraction, see an AR reconstruction of its earlier form, then follow guides and context into the story around it. I owned the product and technology architecture around the app, including local-business and mass-transit integrations. The asset developer built the AR scenes; I made sure the experience connected those scenes to the city around them.

The project was backed by Montenegro's national tourism organization and was discussed in the country's Senate during the COVID era as part of the conversation around tourism technology infrastructure. It came as small businesses suffered a lack of visibility in an increasingly restricted old-towns, and is a concept that has since seen adaptations in many major cities worldwide.

Humanoid Joint Control

The electronics and controls that helped a high efficiency biped keep its legs in check under motion

Project premise

High-precision, low-latency joint measurement is critical to a consistent gait

  • Scope6 high-rate PCBs
  • SystemTik Tok (before that Tik Tok)
  • FocusEncoder accuracy + control scheduling

Tik Tok is an active bio-hybrid humanoid project spun out of Professor Andy Ruina's work at Cornell. I helped design and fabricate the PCBs that captured and relayed rotations at the knees, hips, and ankles. The boards homed multiple subsystems in a fixed packaging, making layout and assembly challenging. Here I learned to use stencil and validation tooling for SMD components, alongside signal integrity and mixed signal logic.

Embedded SW

I worked on real-time control and a distributed scheduler for coordinated rollouts across 6 joint actuation boards. Central compute was FreeRTOS on a stripped Jetson Orin, while networking with the boards was across fault-tolerant CANOpen and EtherCAT. Edge processing was on ARM Cortex-M7 SAM microcontroller.

The payoff was seeing a multi-joint rollout start to resemble a gait. Nothing glamorous about a clean encoder trace, but it is a good day when the whole stack finally agrees on where the body is.

rotary encoder
  → Cortex-M7 embedded procesing
  → CANOpen / EtherCAT communication
  → Nvidia Jetson timestamp alignment
  → multi-joint gait rollout

Cornell Electric Vehicles

Electric vehicles ground-up designed and manufactured in-house for energy efficiency and increasing levels of autonomy

Project premise

For the automotive industry, electrification is still maturing, while autonomy is yet unsolved

  • RoleFull-Team Lead
  • Team Size60 students and faculty across ME, EE, CS, Ops
  • Tenure2021 - 2024

When I joined Cornell Electric Vehicles, it was a team of ~20 and solely purposed for Shell Eco Marathon Americas energy efficiency competition. However, it had already been 10 years since the first mass-market Nissan Leaf (quite full-circle, this is the robotaxi platform of choice for Wayve!), and a few of us felt the team needed a new mandate. My first year was across our sapling Autonomy team, where traditional computer vision and SLAM led us to promising results in sim and virtual competitions (still COVID-era). In parallel however, I learned more about PCB design, and leaned into building the first 'Electrical Automation' board. This board served as what industry calls a 'vehicle interface controller', switching and conducting integrity checks on actuation outputs/feedback from our onboard autonomy compute. This effort paved the way for our eventual L1 autonomy demonstration: Autonomous Emergency Braking. The team has since championed the autonomy stack well past L1, and on vehicles of multiple scales, making autonomy a first-class pursuit of its members.

My Journey

In my second year, I took the role of Electrical Lead and helped grow the overall team to 40. With promising results on both energy efficiency results and autonomy development, I took on Full-Team Lead in my final year. Here, we grew the org to 60, and won numerous accolades both as a Cornell student team and a Shell Eco Marathon competitor. We raised among the highest fundraising sums of the student teams, and began partnering with labs and professors from the Cornell community to buildout our role as a bridge between students and frontier autonomy in industry. Personally, this project led me to Wayve, where I'm now deploying autonomous vehicles to public roads in some of the most complex geographies worldwide!

Most of my engineering time went into 7 revisions of our 2kW BLDC Motor Controller (or 'Electric Speed Controller'). I experimented with everything mixed signal and power here: different power layouts, commutation patterns, heat dissipation, and (most frustratingly) load protections. One of these Motor Controllers ran our 150kg car and driver around the paddocks of the iconic Indianapolis Motor Speedway!

Outside of electrical and autonomy development, the workshop became a second home for me in college, and my teammates a second family. I learned a ton, from carbon fiber resin layups and machining extrusions, to aero profiling and low-latency telemetry. From a birds-eye view of technical roadmaps running all-hands, to the minutia of subteam struggles, some of my best (and most challenging) memories are with the team.

Silverbacks Engineering

Lightweight and finely tuned prototype vehicle for supermileage

Project premise

ICE efficiency is a complex optimization with chassis, engine map, and driving profile all contributing

  • RoleTeam Lead
  • Team12 students
  • ResultTop high-school efficiency result

Many high schools have teams where students explore engineering, but at the International School of The Hague the team was more serious. The Netherlands has a sterling record of high school and university teams competing at the frontiers of energy technology in mobility, particularly across battery-electric and hydrogen fuel sources. The Silverbacks Engineering team I led still holds one of the strongest efficiency results recorded by a high-school team in the Shell Eco Marathon Europe competiton.

On the car

Outside of exercising leadership as a skill, I had two major technical contributions to the team. I designed the steering wheel and its embedded communication system, giving the driver a two-way radio link for contact with the off-track. This included transcribed messages on a display embedded in the wheel, as well as a color coding system for engine modes and status. I also spent much time with our GX35 Honda engine, working on engine mappings for RPM-Torque ranges, in order to maximize volumetric efficiency across our expected driving ranges.

MARTHA

COVID-era service to help avoid a crowd before you even leave the house

Project premise

Social distancing on a college campus was impractical at best, but impossible without remote hotspot tracking

  • BuiltIOS app + aggregation website
  • MilestoneCornell HackOurCampus FInalist
  • DirectionCampus-wide deployment

MARTHA ("Monitoring and Restriction Tracking Hotspot Application", but more importantly named for Cornell President Martha Pollock) was a hackathon solution to the problem of avoiding hotspots for unsafe activity around campus. Dining halls, frat row, and campus buildings were the most obvious places to avoid, but how could you know if your walking route to classes was packed?

The UI

We kept user interfaces simple: an app that serves as an 'opt-in' tracker and live hotspot display for students, and a website that serves as a supervisory view for Campus enforcement/planning to identify long-term trends and patterns in distribution.

While lockdown ended before a solution like this became necessary, Fall 2020 was the perfect time to build and demo the capability of such a service. There are potential post-COVID applications of such opt-in tracking tech, like crowd management for venues, city organizers, or even for public services to track usage.

SAFE

Sensor pod and paired mobile app to detect and prevent heat stress early

Project premise

In-home heatstroke is becoming a major health hazard for the elderly in developing-world households

  • BuiltSensor array + AI model
  • PilotIndia field test
  • ResearchModel input nullification handling

SAFE was a concept inspired by the heatstroke of an elderly relative in their home in India. It sounds like an isolated incident, and not one we usually hear of in the West, but is surprisingly common in the East, particularly among the elderly. The research is that bodily thermal regulation capability reduces with age, as well as the ability to accurately gauge enviornmental conditions. Combined with rising temperatures globally, and the liklihood of households in developing countries to lack central climate control, SAFE was intended to act as a canary for unsafe enviornmental conditions.

How it worked

In the 3D-printed pod was a host of ambient climate sensors and a cellular-equipped microcontroller. As a pod is first plugged into the wall, a unique identification key is generated, allowing pairing with a mobile app. From here, the pod consumes ~500mW of power and passively logs enviornmental conditions to the cloud. In the cloud my SAFE regression model, trained on public thermoregulatory incident databases, would estimate the suitability of the enviornment for the given elderly profile provided via the app. As a result, a dynamic risk assessment is provided to the holder of the app, for them to act on as guided by generated recommendations.

To trial the system, I took a handful of pods to India and presented to an esteemed geriatric physician, who oversaw my successful trials in a handful of old-age homes. Should the system have been taken forward and productionised, it could have application in senior homes and hospitals everywhere.

One interesting failure mode I noted from the India field trials was due to missing inputs (mainly sensor drop-outs), causing the model to fail forward passes and provide inaccurate risk assessments. This prompted my research on neural network input nullification for healthcare applications, and proposal on a heuristic to reduce the impact of missing data. The resulting paper was presented in a talk to University of Amsterdam faculty.
