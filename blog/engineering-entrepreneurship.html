Engineering entrepreneurship

Notes on product thinking, customer discovery, risk, and iteration.

Building a company often resembles engineering: name the unknown, make a small test, pay attention to what changes, then decide what to try next. The work becomes clearer when a question is small enough to answer in the real world.

Early on, it is easy to confuse momentum with learning. A full calendar, a polished deck, or a growing backlog can feel like progress. None of those replace a conversation, a prototype, or a decision that puts an assumption at risk.

That is where an engineering habit helps. Start with a specific belief, choose the cheapest honest way to test it, then look closely at the result. The point is not to make each test succeed. It is to reduce uncertainty before committing more time, money, or attention.

Customer discovery works the same way. The useful conversations are not the ones where people praise an idea; they are the ones that reveal how they already solve the problem, what they have tried, and where the current approach breaks down.

Over time, those observations compound into product judgment. A product is not simply a collection of features. It is a sequence of choices about what matters, what can wait, and what evidence is strong enough to act on.

There is no clean separation between engineering and entrepreneurship in that process. Both ask for enough rigor to learn from reality, plus enough optimism to build before every answer is known.
